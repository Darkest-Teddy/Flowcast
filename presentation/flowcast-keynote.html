Flowcast

See how busy campus is before you go.

Hourly crowd forecasts for Boston University's libraries, dining halls and gyms.

Hackachusetts · Civic Action track

Open cold, no preamble. Read the headline, then stop talking for a beat. Everyone in the room has stood in a line they could have avoided.

Do not introduce the team or the tech yet. The first ninety seconds belong to the problem.

01   The setting

A campus is a small city that nobody runs like one.

48,000
people sharing the same sidewalks, dining halls and libraries — 36,977 students and 11,140 staff
101
places across the Charles River, Fenway and Medical campuses
1
avenue most of them walk down to get anywhere

Frame the scale before the pain. A city of this size would have traffic engineers, published occupancy and a planning department. A campus has none of that, even though its population moves far more predictably.

Say the student number out loud as a person-count, not a statistic: thirty-seven thousand people, all trying to eat inside the same ninety minutes.

02   12:20 PM

You have fifty minutes to eat.

You walk ten minutes to the dining hall. The line is out the door. You walk back with a granola bar and eat it in a hallway before your 1:25.

Nothing warned you. Nothing could have.

This is the whole pitch in one anecdote. Keep it in second person and keep it specific — ten minutes, the hallway, the 1:25. Judges have lived it.

Resist adding a second example. One concrete scene beats three abstract ones.

03   The problem is measured, not imagined

This is not a feeling. It is a count.

BU already counts this. It files the numbers with the City of Boston. Crowding is not the discovery here.

This slide buys you credibility. We are not asserting a problem; we are citing BU's own Article 80 filings and reports. Name the source out loud once — "BU's own Warren Towers filing with the City" — so nobody thinks these are our estimates.

The point to land: everybody already knows campus is crowded, and it is measurable to the person. So the gap is not awareness.

04   And almost all the data is already public

BU publishes nearly everything you would need.

  • 5,904class meetings, each with a time and a roomBU course catalog
  • 437classrooms with published seat countsBU classroom directory
  • 24study spaces, with amenities, hours and booking limitsBU Libraries
  • 88public campus events, each with a venueTerrier Central
  • 101places with posted opening hoursBU Dining, Libraries, Housing, FitRec

All free. No login. Scattered across dozens of separate pages, none of which is shaped like an hour of your day.

The turn in the argument. Pause before this slide. The audience expects "and there's no data" — give them the opposite.

Emphasise "no login". Everything on this list is already paid for by the university and already public. Nobody has to be convinced to release anything.

05   Except for exactly one thing

Live building occupancy, study-seat counts, room bookings. Unavailable. — Flowcast source audit, 25 October 2026

BU publishes the seats. It cannot publish whether anyone is sitting in them. There is no occupancy feed — not on this campus, and not on almost any other.

This is the problem slide. Everything before it was setup. Say it plainly: the one number that would answer "should I walk over there?" is the only number nobody has.

If asked why BU does not just install sensors: cost, privacy and retrofit across 101 buildings. That is exactly why a schedule-based projection is the practical route — it needs no new hardware and no new data collection about real people.

We know this gap precisely because we audited every source and wrote down what was missing. That audit is in the repo.

06   So what does everyone actually do?

They check the only way you can. They go and look.

Thirty-seven thousand people, solving the same question separately, all day, with their feet — every meal, every study session, every trip to the gym.

The information to answer it in advance already exists. It is simply not in one place, and not in the shape of an hour.

Name the cost without inventing a number for it. We deliberately do not claim "X hours wasted per student" — we have not measured that, and a judge will catch an invented figure.

The honest cost statement: the same question is being answered thousands of times a day by the most expensive possible method, when the inputs to answer it cheaply are already published.

07   And when it matters far more than lunch

A building empties. Where do three thousand people go?

A fire drill, an alarm, a closure. Safety and facilities staff plan for it on paper, because nobody can watch it happen before it happens.

This is the same question as the lunch rush, asked at a much worse moment.

This widens the problem from student convenience to civic infrastructure — which is the track we are in. Do not oversell it: we are not claiming to predict emergencies, only to let planners rehearse one against a realistic campus.

Link the two halves explicitly: the same model that tells a student where to eat tells a safety officer where the overflow lands. One engine, two very different stakes.

08   The insight

You do not need sensors. You need the schedule.

Where people will be is mostly decided in advance — by class times, room assignments, posted hours and event venues. All of it published. Put it together and the crowd becomes a calculation instead of a measurement.

The pivot from problem to solution. Deliver this as the obvious-in-hindsight line it is.

Why this works on a campus specifically: unlike a shopping street, a campus population is scheduled. Thirty-seven thousand people have told the university in advance where they will be at 10 AM. That is the structural fact the product rests on.

09   Flowcast

A live 3D model of the whole campus.

36,977 simulated students follow BU's real published class schedules across 101 places, so anyone can see which dining halls, libraries, gyms and buildings will be packed, hour by hour.

  • Quiet under 40%
  • Busy 40–85%
  • Packed 85%+

Free. No login. Runs in a browser.

Say the student count precisely — 36,977, not "about 37,000". It matches BU's published Fall 2025 census by level and full/part-time status, and the precision signals that the roster was built, not guessed.

Point at the three chips. Every crowd level in the product always carries a word, a percentage and a people icon — never colour alone. That is an accessibility decision, and it comes up again later.

11   Demo · event impact

"If I go in on Thursday, what will be different?"

Pick any date. See that day's events and, for every place, hour-by-hour crowding with and without them.

The third figure is the one to dwell on. Events whose venue is login-only or unmapped are shown anyway, with the reason. We list our own coverage gaps in the product's interface, not just in the README.

During an event, students who are free attend, up to the room's published seat count — never beyond it. Registrations are never treated as attendance.

12   Demo · incidents

Rehearse the drill before you run it.

Start a fire drill, alarm or closure on any building. Occupants evacuate, gather, then choose a feasible next place — one that is open, in range and has room. Classes stay where they are scheduled; meals and study move.

Everything is compared against the same day without the incident, so planners can see exactly where the overflow goes.

This is the slide for the civic and safety judges. Demo a drill on a big building — Warren or CDS — and point at the two or three places that absorb the people.

State the limit before you are asked: these are scenarios, not operational predictions. We have no observed evacuation measurements to calibrate against, and the product says so.

13   Demo · for one student

"Least crowded library right now."

Ask in plain language and the answer comes from the same simulation and the same walking routes the map uses. Alongside it: today's schedule, forecasts for your places, and a leave-by time for your next class.

Walking times come from saved campus routes at 1.4 m/s, rounded up to whole minutes, plus a five-minute arrival buffer. Unmapped buildings get no guessed departure time.

Ask one question live. Good ones: "quietest dining hall at 6 PM", "how far is CS 132 from DS 340?" Then follow up — the chat keeps context.

Privacy line, say it here: questions about your own schedule are always answered on the device. Your schedule never goes to the server.

15   And where it is wrong

The biggest gap is the one we publish.

37%
FitRec on a weekday: 1,465 simulated against 3,913 measured. The measured figure counts every pass holder; we simulate students only, and BU's 11,140 employees are not in the model yet.
#1
which is why adding staff and visitors as a separate, clearly labelled layer is the first item on our roadmap — not a footnote in it.

A civic tool that hides its misses is not a civic tool.

Volunteer the worst number. Judges will trust everything else on the previous slide far more once you have shown them this one, unprompted.

The gaps are not embarrassing — they are a work plan. Each one points at a specific next step, and every step is additive rather than a rewrite.

16   Honest by design

Published evidence and model assumptions never mix.

  • Published

    Measured, sourced, checksummed

    Class times, seat counts, posted hours, bed counts, event venues. Every value keeps its source URL, retrieval time and SHA-256, so any number on screen can be traced back to the page it came from.

  • Modeled

    Named, tunable, labelled as such

    Enrollment, meals, study time, event attendance. Each is a named constant with a plain-language entry in the simulation's assumptions list, and the interface says which is which.

Never invented: no made-up venue, capacity or coordinate. Registrations are never attendance. Private events are skipped, not parsed. Records we cannot resolve stay unmapped and say so, rather than becoming a plausible-looking number.

This is the Responsibility and Ethics answer, and it is a design rule enforced in the codebase, not a promise in a slide. Steering rules in the repo make it non-negotiable for every session.

The line to land: if a source fails or lacks a field, the application shows the limitation. Adding another plausible number is not a replacement for an integration.

17   Who it is for

One model. Five very different questions.

  • StudentsAvoid the rush, find a quiet seat, see how an event changes a building, plan an accessible walk.
  • ClubsPick a room and an hour that will not collide with peak crowds.
  • Dining & librariesAn hour-by-hour picture of expected demand, built only from pages they already publish.
  • SafetyRehearse drills and closures, and see where thousands of people go next.
  • Boston & the MBTAStudent flow onto Comm Ave sidewalks, Green Line stops and Bluebikes docks.

Do not read all five. Name students, then jump to safety and the City — the distance between those two is the argument that this is infrastructure, not an app.

For students with anxiety, sensory sensitivities or mobility needs, knowing where it is quiet is the difference between going and staying home. Say that one.

18   Beyond BU

Every input is something campuses already publish.

Course schedules, room capacities, opening hours, event calendars, public transit feeds. The pipeline records where each value came from, so another campus swaps in its own pages rather than rebuilding the model.

The same approach fits any public venue with a schedule: hospitals, convention centres, stadium districts.

Keep this short — it is a scope claim, not a business plan. The strength is that it requires no new agreement, no new hardware and no new data about people: only pages that are already public.

If pressed on what porting actually costs: the collectors are per-source Python modules with recorded provenance, so a new campus is new collectors against the same simulation.

19   Responsibility

Nobody is tracked. Nobody is guessed at.

  • Synthetic people

    No real student is modelled

    The 36,977 students are generated to match BU's published census. Not one of them represents or is derived from a real person, and no real location is ever tracked.

  • Nothing private

    Public pages only

    No login-only or personal data is collected. Private events are skipped rather than parsed. Your own schedule stays in your session, and schedule questions are answered on your device.

  • Readable by everyone

    Never colour alone

    Every crowd level carries a word, a percentage and a one, two or three-person icon, at 4.5:1 contrast. Full keyboard support, and the map honours reduced-motion settings.

The ethics slide is worth real points and takes thirty seconds. The central claim: a crowd-forecasting tool is exactly the kind of product that would normally be built on location surveillance — and this one is built on timetables instead.

That is a design choice, not a limitation. We get the same answer without collecting anything about anyone.

20   How it is built

Public pages in. A deterministic model out.

  1. 01 · Collect Python pipeline Cached, rate-limited collectors scrape and normalise public BU, MBTA and City of Boston pages.
  2. 02 · Commit Checksummed snapshots Every value keeps its source URL, retrieval time and SHA-256. The whole build runs offline from these.
  3. 03 · Simulate TypeScript model Enrols students in real sections within seat limits and plans seven days around hours, capacity and walking time.
  4. 04 · Show Map and dashboard GPU-instanced walkers and crowd glow on MapLibre and deck.gl, plus a React dashboard.

Deterministic: random choices are stable hashes keyed by student and activity, so the same inputs always give the same campus and one change never reshuffles unrelated students. 522 tests, including property-based event-day tests that caught a real bug where events in classrooms could push a building over capacity.

Thirty seconds, no longer. Determinism is the detail worth saying aloud: it is what makes the forecasts reproducible and the validation above meaningful.

Built with Kiro's spec-driven workflow — EARS requirements, steering rules that enforce the data-honesty policy on every session, and agent hooks that fail a commit when a data snapshot changes without its checksum.
